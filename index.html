What randomness actually means

A result is random when nobody — not you, not us, not anyone — can predict it better than chance before it happens. That sounds simple, but it is a surprisingly slippery idea. A shuffled deck of cards is random because every order is equally likely and no one arranged it. A number you "just think of" is usually not random at all: people asked to pick a number between one and ten choose seven far more often than chance would suggest, and almost nobody picks one or ten. Genuine randomness means every possible outcome has exactly the probability it should, and each draw is independent of the ones before it — the generator has no memory and no favourites.

That independence is where most intuition goes wrong. If a coin lands heads five times in a row, tails is not "due"; the coin does not know what happened before. Random sequences naturally contain streaks and clusters, and a sequence that looks too tidy — heads, tails, heads, tails, perfectly alternating — is actually one of the least likely patterns of all. We explore these ideas properly in What Is a Random Number? and in Random vs. Pseudorandom, which explains the difference between true randomness and the very convincing imitation most computers produce.

Everyday uses of random numbers

Random numbers quietly run a large part of modern life. Lottery draws depend on them, which is why our article on how lotteries use random numbers looks at the machines and audits that keep draws honest. Teachers use generators to pick a student fairly instead of always calling on the front row. Giveaway hosts draw a winning comment or raffle ticket without favouritism. Tabletop gamers roll dice, developers shuffle playlists and test software with random inputs, and researchers assign volunteers to trial groups at random so results cannot be skewed — a technique called random sampling that underpins almost every opinion poll and medical study you have ever read about.

Behind the scenes, randomness is also a security tool. Every time your browser opens a secure connection, random numbers help build the encryption keys that protect it. Password resets, one-time login codes and two-factor prompts all start life as random values that an attacker must be unable to guess. That is exactly why the tools on this page draw from a cryptographic source rather than a simple formula: the numbers are suitable not just for games, but for jobs where predictability would be a real problem. Our deep dive on how computers generate randomness explains where that unpredictability comes from.

How this generator works

When you press the Generate button, the page asks your browser for random bytes through a built-in browser feature called crypto.getRandomValues. Your operating system gathers tiny unpredictable signals — timing jitter, hardware noise, device activity — and mixes them into a pool of randomness that this function draws from. The page then converts those random bytes into numbers inside your chosen range using a technique called rejection sampling: any byte value that would make some outcomes slightly more likely than others is thrown away and redrawn, so every number in your range has precisely the same chance of appearing. If you switch off duplicates, the generator keeps a list of what it has already drawn and redraws any repeat, the same way you would pull raffle tickets out of a hat without putting them back.

Everything happens locally in your browser. Your settings and results are never sent to a server, stored in a database, or shared with anyone — you can read more in our privacy policy. The dice roller and coin flip use the identical random source and the same rejection-sampling care, so a six is exactly as likely as a one, and heads exactly as likely as tails, on every single try.

A short note on fairness

No generator can promise that a short run of results will "look" random. Generate five numbers between one and ten and you might well get two of them the same, or three in a row — that is what real randomness looks like up close. Fairness is a property of the process over many draws, not of any single handful. If you ever doubt a tool like this, the honest test is volume: generate a few thousand numbers, count how often each value appears, and check the counts are roughly even, with the small wobbles getting proportionally smaller as the sample grows. That wobble-shrinking behaviour is the law of large numbers, and it is also why a lottery needs millions of players before its results settle into the expected pattern described in our lottery article.

What Is a Random Number?

RandomNumberHub Guides · Reading time: about 5 minutes

Ask a room full of people to each write down a random number between one and ten, and something odd happens. The results cluster. Seven is picked far more than any other value, three and eight do well too, and almost nobody chooses one or ten. The numbers feel random to the people writing them, but they are not: a good guesser who knows these habits can predict the room's choices far better than chance. That gap — between what feels random and what actually is random — is the whole subject of this article.

Two requirements: unpredictability and fairness

A random draw has to satisfy two conditions. First, it must be unpredictable: before the draw happens, nobody can know the outcome, or influence it, or guess it with better odds than the probabilities allow. Second, it must be fair: every possible outcome occurs with exactly its intended probability. For a single number between one and ten, fair means each value shows up about one time in ten over the long run. The human number-pickers fail on both counts — their choices can be predicted better than chance, and the distribution is lopsided.

There is a third condition that is easy to miss: independence. Each draw must be unaffected by the draws before it. A fair coin that lands heads does not become more likely to land tails next time; its probability stays at exactly one half. Streaks of five or six heads in a row feel suspicious, but in a long sequence of flips they are not just possible, they are expected. A sequence with no streaks at all would be the truly suspicious one.

Why randomness looks wrong

Human brains are pattern-finding machines, and randomness is full of patterns by accident. Flip a coin twenty times and you will probably see a run of three or four identical results somewhere in the list. Roll two dice and the totals bunch toward the middle, because there are more ways to make a seven than a two — our article on dice probability basics shows the full breakdown. Shuffle a playlist and two songs by the same artist land back to back, and listeners complain the shuffle is "not random enough", when in fact a perfectly even spread would be the artificial result. Streaming services have famously adjusted their shuffles to feel random rather than be random.

Where random numbers come from

Physical sources — tumbling dice, shuffled cards, lottery balls bouncing in an air-mix machine — get their unpredictability from physics: tiny differences in how a die leaves your hand grow into completely different outcomes. Computers have the opposite problem, because they are built to follow instructions exactly. They solve it two ways: by measuring unpredictable physical events (the timing of keystrokes, electrical noise, hardware jitter) and by using algorithms whose output passes every statistical test for randomness even though it starts from a starting value called a seed. The difference between those two approaches is the subject of Random vs. Pseudorandom, and How Computers Generate Randomness explains how the generator on this site's homepage turns your browser's built-in cryptographic source into fair numbers in any range you choose.

A practical definition

For everyday purposes, a random number is one produced by a process where every outcome in the range is equally likely, each draw is independent, and nobody can predict or steer the result. The tools on RandomNumberHub are built to that standard: they draw from a cryptographic source and reject any intermediate value that would tilt the odds, however slightly. Whether you are picking a raffle winner, choosing a sample, or just deciding who makes the tea, that is the standard worth insisting on — because the moment a draw can be predicted or nudged, it stops being a draw and starts being a decision in disguise.

How Computers Generate Randomness

RandomNumberHub Guides · Reading time: about 6 minutes

A computer is the worst imaginable place to look for randomness. Machines are built to do exactly what they are told, the same way, every time: give a program the same input and it must return the same output, or something is broken. Yet computers shuffle our music, deal our cards, encrypt our messages and pick our lottery numbers. The story of how a deterministic machine produces unpredictable numbers is one of the quiet triumphs of modern computing.

Step one: find some chaos

Every random system starts with entropy — genuine unpredictability harvested from the physical world. Operating systems collect it constantly: the microsecond timing between your keystrokes, the movement of your mouse, tiny fluctuations in disk and network timing, electrical noise inside hardware sensors. None of these can be predicted or replayed. They are stirred together into an "entropy pool", a reservoir of disorder that the system guards carefully and tops up whenever it can. Some specialised hardware goes further, measuring thermal noise or even radioactive decay, but for most devices the everyday jitter of a busy machine is plenty.

Step two: stretch it with mathematics

Entropy arrives slowly and irregularly, but programs want random numbers quickly and in bulk. So the pool is used to seed a cryptographic generator — an algorithm designed so that its output is indistinguishable from true randomness to anyone who does not know the internal state, and so that seeing a long stream of outputs reveals nothing about the next one or the ones before. These generators are held to a brutal standard, because they protect real secrets: encryption keys, session tokens and password-reset links all begin as their output. If the generator were predictable, the security built on it would collapse.

What your browser provides

Modern browsers expose this machinery to web pages through a function called crypto.getRandomValues, which fills a block of memory with bytes from the operating system's cryptographic generator. That is precisely what the random number generator on RandomNumberHub uses. One subtlety matters: turning random bytes into, say, a number from one to ten must be done carefully. The naive approach — take a random byte and divide — can leave some outcomes a fraction more likely than others, because 256 does not divide evenly by ten. The fix, called rejection sampling, is to discard any byte in the uneven remainder and draw again. The bias removed is tiny, but fairness is exactly the kind of thing worth being fussy about.

What about ordinary "random" functions?

Most programming languages also ship a simpler random function, designed for games and simulations, whose output is produced by a fast formula from a seed value. Those are pseudorandom: perfectly fine for shuffling a playlist, unacceptable for generating a password, because anyone who learns the seed or observes enough output can reconstruct the sequence. The distinction is important enough that we gave it its own article: Random vs. Pseudorandom. The short version is that this site deliberately uses the cryptographic kind — the strong stuff — even though picking a raffle winner does not strictly require it. There is no reason to settle for less when your device already does the hard work.

Dice Probability Basics

RandomNumberHub Guides · Reading time: about 5 minutes

Dice are the oldest random number generators in the world — versions of them have been found in ancient Egyptian tombs and Indus Valley ruins — and they remain the friendliest way to learn probability. Everything about dice odds follows from one idea: count the equally likely outcomes, and see how many of them are the one you want.

One die: the simplest case

A single fair die has six faces, each equally likely, so the probability of any specific face is one in six — about 16.7%. The probability of rolling an even number is three in six, or 50%, because three of the faces (two, four, six) qualify. Every dice calculation, however complicated the game, is this same counting exercise scaled up. You can test it with the dice roller on our homepage: roll one die a few hundred times and each face settles near a sixth of the total.

Two dice: why seven is king

With two dice there are 36 equally likely combinations (six faces on the first die times six on the second), but the totals are not equally likely at all. There is only one way to make a total of two — double one — and only one way to make twelve. A total of seven, by contrast, can be made six different ways: 1+6, 2+5, 3+4, 4+3, 5+2 and 6+1. That is why seven is the most common result in dice games, appearing in about 16.7% of rolls, and why totals get rarer as they move toward the extremes.

More dice, smoother curves

Add a third die and the bunching toward the middle gets stronger: totals of ten and eleven dominate, while three and eighteen become rare one-in-216 events. This is the beginning of one of the deepest patterns in statistics — add up enough independent random values and the totals arrange themselves into a bell shape, clustered around the average. It is the same reason heights and measurement errors form bell curves in nature. The average total also becomes predictable: each die contributes an average of 3.5, so two dice average seven and five dice average 17.5, even though no single roll can be forecast.

The mistake to avoid

The most common dice fallacy is believing past rolls change future odds. Ten rolls without a six do not make a six "due" — the dice have no memory, a point we return to in What Is a Random Number? Each roll starts fresh at one in six. Probability tells you what to expect over many rolls; it promises nothing about the next one, and any system claiming otherwise is selling you a story, not mathematics.

Random Numbers in Lotteries

RandomNumberHub Guides · Reading time: about 5 minutes

A lottery is a promise: millions of people hand over money on the understanding that the winning numbers will be chosen by a process nobody can predict, influence or dispute. Keeping that promise in public, week after week, under the eyes of players, regulators and television cameras, turns out to be a serious engineering and auditing exercise.

Two ways to pick the numbers

Most large lotteries use one of two methods. The traditional approach is a mechanical draw machine: a set of numbered balls, all manufactured to identical size and weight, tumbled in a transparent chamber by paddles or blasted around by jets of air until one is ejected. The physical chaos is the point — as with dice, microscopic differences at the start make the outcome unforeseeable. The second method is a certified random number generator: software, usually fed by a hardware entropy source, that produces the winning combination digitally. Digital draws are cheaper and easier to run frequently, which is why many daily and online games use them.

How fairness is proven, not just claimed

Because the stakes are high, lotteries wrap the draw in layers of verification. Ball sets are weighed, measured and stored under lock, and the set used is often chosen at random on the day. Machines are tested with rehearsal draws before the real one. Independent auditors — typically an outside accounting or testing firm — observe the process and certify the result. For digital draws, the generator software is tested by accredited laboratories against statistical standards, and its code and seed handling are kept under strict controls. Draws are broadcast live or recorded under supervision so the public can see there was no second take.

Why your "lucky numbers" do not help

Every combination in a fair lottery has exactly the same chance, whether it is 1-2-3-4-5-6 or a scattered handful of birthdays. Combinations like 1-2-3-4-5-6 feel impossible because they look ordered, but the draw machine cannot see patterns — it only produces sequences, each as likely as the next. The one real strategic effect runs the other way: popular combinations (birthdays, sequences, patterns on the ticket grid) are picked by many players, so if one of them wins, the prize is split more ways. Choosing unpopular numbers does not raise your chance of winning; it only raises your expected share if you do. The mathematics behind that distinction is the same counting logic explained in dice probability basics, scaled up to millions of combinations.

Quick picks and trust

When you ask a terminal for a "quick pick", a random generator chooses your numbers for you — and in many large lotteries, quick picks account for the majority of tickets and a proportional share of jackpot winners, exactly as fairness predicts. Players sometimes worry that a computerised pick could be steered, but a certified generator drawing from genuine entropy is, if anything, harder to tamper with than a physical machine. To understand what "genuine entropy" means inside a machine, read How Computers Generate Randomness. The generator on our homepage uses the same class of cryptographic source as a quick-pick terminal — though, sadly, we cannot sell you a ticket.

Random vs. Pseudorandom: What Is the Difference?

RandomNumberHub Guides · Reading time: about 5 minutes

Most of the "random" numbers in your life are fakes — excellent, useful, carefully engineered fakes. Understanding the difference between true randomness and its imitation, pseudorandomness, explains a lot: why your music shuffle repeats an artist, why a video game can be "seeded", and why generating a password demands a stronger tool than shuffling a playlist.

Pseudorandom: a formula pretending

A pseudorandom number generator is an algorithm — a fixed recipe. You give it a starting value called a seed, and it churns out a sequence of numbers that looks random: no obvious pattern, evenly spread, passing statistical tests. But the sequence is completely determined by the seed. Start the same generator with the same seed and you get the same sequence, digit for digit, forever. That reproducibility is a feature in many jobs: a scientist can rerun a simulation exactly, a game can recreate a world from a short seed code, and a developer can debug a failure by replaying the "random" events that caused it.

True random: measured, not calculated

A true random generator does not calculate its numbers; it measures them from a physical process nobody can predict — electronic noise, atmospheric static, the timing jitter inside hardware, in exotic cases the decay of radioactive atoms or photons bouncing off a half-silvered mirror. Because nothing is calculated, there is no seed to steal and no sequence to replay. The output is only as good as the measurement, and gathering it is slower, which is why true randomness is usually used sparingly: to seed and reseed the faster generators, including the cryptographic ones described in How Computers Generate Randomness.

The middle ground that protects you

Between the two sits the cryptographically secure pseudorandom generator. It is still an algorithm, but one designed so that even an attacker who watches its output for years cannot predict what comes next or work backwards to earlier values, provided its seed came from real entropy and stays secret. This is the category your browser's crypto.getRandomValues belongs to — and therefore the category the tools on RandomNumberHub belong to. For picking raffle winners and rolling virtual dice it is honestly more strength than required; for anything touching security, it is the minimum acceptable.

Which kind do you actually need?

For games, simulations, shuffling and sampling, plain pseudorandom is fine and fast. For passwords, encryption keys, login codes, gambling and draws with money attached, insist on cryptographic or true randomness — predictability there is not a quirk, it is a vulnerability. And for the deepest question underneath all of this — what randomness even means when a sequence looks patternless — start with What Is a Random Number?
